VERTICAL BIBLE. The 60 to 90 second micro-drama layer. Twenty-two rules, mined from six craft theory books and collided against the fiction library so nothing here restates a document you already own.

HOW TO USE THIS. Not a reading order. Four entry points.

Auditing a draft: run PART TWO against every episode, then PART THREE against the gate, then PART SIX against any beat the coverage says did not land.
Drafting an episode: rules 6, 7, 8, 9. Nothing else, and in that order.
Testing hooks: PART ONE, then the HOOK SPRINT SHEET.
Building or fixing a season: rules 10, 14, 15, 18, then the EPISODE GRID.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT COVER. Line-level prose is VOICE RULES. Scene execution inside a unit, including physical business, threat construction and interruption rationing, is CRAFT RULES. Where a rule below stops short and points at one of those, that is deliberate: the clause lives there and is not repeated here.

PART ONE. THE FIRST THREE SECONDS.

1. NAME THE WANT IN THE FIRST LINE WHEN. The opening of any episode, above all episode one. DO. Make the first spoken line an accusation, a demand with a deadline, or a price. Somebody is trying to get something and the line says what. Deliver identity, relationship and location later, inside the argument, never before it. STOPS. Without a stated objective the viewer cannot assemble what she is looking at, and unassembled footage is exactly what the thumb clears. Faces and production value do not substitute. The goal is what lets the brain file the image, and the completion rate never recovers no matter how good second twenty is. EXAMPLE (esports). Before: interior, team house, morning. Mei walks in with her gear bag and takes in the empty desks. After: "Your name is off the roster. Sign the buyout by noon or we void your visa."

2. OPEN ON THE TAKING WHEN. Any episode a cold viewer might land on, which is all of them after episode three. DO. Show one person giving something up and another person taking it, on camera, before you explain who either of them is. Nothing in the first three seconds may require an earlier episode. Hold the charge that depends on knowing these people for seconds five to ten. STOPS. Outrage lands before comprehension does, which makes it the only reaction that reliably survives a three-second window. Sympathy built through backstory arrives after the swipe, and a beat that only pays if you watched episode nine offers nothing to the person meeting the show at episode ten. EXAMPLE (depot). Before: we meet Dani, twenty-six, exhausted, pulling doubles to keep her sister in school. After: Dani hands the foreman the overtime slips for the whole crew. He signs one, his own, and drops the rest in the bin while she is still standing there.

3. RUN THE REACTION BEFORE THE CAUSE WHEN. Opening on an emotional beat rather than a physical one. DO. Start on a face already reacting and let the line that caused it arrive second. STOPS. A cause delivered first is setup, and setup is what the thumb clears. BOUNDARY. The general ban on arrivals, waking, and looking around is VOICE RULES 8. Laying the incoming line over the outgoing image so it misreads is CRAFT RULES 25. This rule owns only the inversion. EXAMPLE (wedding). Before: interior kitchen. The mother-in-law enters, looks around, says so this is where you live. After: close on the bride, tears already falling, mouth open. Then the mother-in-law's voice off screen, finishing: and you will sign it before the ceremony.

4. OPEN THE GAP, THEN BOOK ITS CLOSING WHEN. Episode one, and every time you plant a secret. DO. Hand the audience the fact the protagonist is missing and withhold it only from the characters. At the moment you plant it, fix the episode number where that character learns it, and stage that episode as a climax of its own, built so the viewer watches a face arrive at what she already knows. STOPS. Curiosity takes longer than three seconds to form. Superiority is instant. And an irony debt left open is the one payoff the viewer's investment actually earned: skip it or summarise it and she leaves at exactly the episode you needed her to pay. EXAMPLE (transplant unit). Before: a woman signs for a delivery. Card: WHOSE BABY IS IT? After: card: THE MAN AT HER DOOR BURIED HER HUSBAND LAST TUESDAY. She opens it and smiles at him. Episode 40, fixed now: she opens the file at her own desk and finds her signature under a swap she never made. Nothing else happens in that episode.

5. PLAY THE SYMPTOM, BANK THE CAUSE WHEN. Introducing a lead. DO. Show the behaviour the wound produced. She will not let anyone stand behind her, she pays cash only, she checks every exit before she takes off her coat. Keep the origin out of the free block entirely. STOPS. Front-loading the flashback spends your only long-range question before anyone has paid, and everything after it is consequence with nothing withheld. EXAMPLE (night ward). Before: open on the childhood fire, then cut to her adult life on the night shift. After: open on her walking the ward and counting doors. Nobody explains it for nine episodes.

PART TWO. THE EPISODE AND ITS EXIT.

6. NAME BOTH CHARGES OR MERGE THE EPISODE WHEN. Drafting or auditing any 60 to 90 second episode. DO. Write the opening condition and the closing condition as one word each. Trusted to suspect. Wanted to disposable. Safe to owned. If the two words match, merge the episode into its neighbour. STOPS. Filler episodes that only move information read as stalling and kill conversion at the gate, because the viewer has learned that an episode can contain nothing. EXAMPLE (office). Before: ep 14, Mei searches the office for the file. Ep 15, Mei keeps searching. After: ep 14 opens trusted, closes suspect. She finds the file and the badge reader logs her name.

7. FLIP THE RANKING IN FRONT OF WITNESSES WHEN. The turn of every episode. DO. Fix who outranks whom at the top, move it by the end, and stage the move in front of the people whose opinion the loser needs. STOPS. Without the flip the episode has events and no result, and the viewer reports that nothing happened. Without witnesses the fall reads as private bad luck, and nobody unlocks the next episode to watch private bad luck. EXAMPLE (wrestling). Before: backstage, alone, the promoter tells Kell he is off the card. Kell takes it hard. After: the promoter kills Kell's music in front of the whole locker room and hands the spot to the rookie. Kell says nothing, picks up his bag, and the rookie holds the door for him.

8. HAND THE EPISODE TO WHOEVER WANTS SOMETHING WHEN. An episode has the lead present but wanting nothing urgent, or waiting for news. DO. Give the episode to whoever's want makes it happen, usually the opponent or a minor character on a private errand, and build it out of their pursuit while the lead absorbs the pressure. STOPS. Reaction has no engine. An episode where the lead only responds ends at the value it began at, which is the definition of a scroll. EXAMPLE (audit). Before: ep 24, Hana waits in the office for news about the audit. After: ep 24, the CFO has ninety minutes to move the second ledger before the auditor's car reaches the lobby, and he needs Hana's keycard to do it.

9. CANCEL THE PLAN, LEAVE ONE WRONG ANSWER WHEN. The last three seconds of any episode. DO. End on a new fact that invalidates the hero's plan: who someone is, what someone owns, who signed, who is alive. Deliver it as one line or one image. Then check it against the second test: the viewer must be able to name a specific next answer, and that answer must be available and wrong. If the fact settles the question, it is a reveal and it ends the pull. If it names nothing, it is a blackout and confusion has no pull at all. STOPS. An episode that ends on feeling reads as finished, the viewer's need closes and the next tap does not happen. A reveal closes it faster. The craving exists only in the narrow band where the viewer believes she has already worked it out. BOUNDARY. Cut before any reaction. That clause is PROSE RULES 1 and is not restated here. EXAMPLE (storm chasing). Before: the radio cuts out. Black screen. End of episode. After: the radio cuts out. Through the windscreen, her husband's truck parked dead in the path of it, driver's door hanging open, and on the seat the ring he swore he lost.

10. END THE LINE ON THE HIT WHEN. The last line before any cut, above all an episode exit. DO. Put the strongest word or image in final position. Move names, hedges and qualifying clauses to the front or cut them. End the line at the hit, not after it. STOPS. Trailing words bleed the button. The thumb starts moving during the wind-down and the exit lands on nothing. EXAMPLE. Before: "I already mailed the invitations to your mother, Priya, which you would know if you ever checked anything." After: "Priya. Your mother already has the invitations."

PART THREE. THE GATE.

11. ALTERNATE THE SIGN AGAINST ONE SEASON QUESTION WHEN. Ordering episodes. DO. Write the season question as one yes or no. Does he ever admit in public that she is his wife. Label every episode plus or minus as an answer to that exact question. Never run two of the same sign back to back, with one exemption: a second same-sign exit is allowed only when it is a different kind of bad, never a larger amount of the same bad. STOPS. Two plus episodes in a row and the viewer feels finished. Two minus and she feels punished. Both close the app on a settled feeling, and by the third repetition of one charge the audience laughs at what you wrote to be devastating. EXAMPLE. Before: ep 20 he is fired, ep 21 he is evicted, ep 22 his dog is taken. After: ep 20 he is fired, ep 21 the rival hands him a car and a contract, ep 22 the contract names his daughter as collateral.

12. SPEND THE POSTER BEFORE THE GATE WHEN. Deciding what goes in the free run. DO. Put the scenes the premise promises inside the free episodes. She serves dinner to the family that evicted her. He watches her run his boardroom. Gate on what happens next. Arrival, setup and paperwork do not count as delivery. STOPS. If the free episodes only explain the premise, you are asking the viewer to pay to start the show. She pays to continue one, never to start one. EXAMPLE. Before: eps 1 to 8, she loses the house, sees the ad, takes the job, meets the family, gets the uniform. Gate. After: by ep 4 she is holding the tray for the sister who evicted her and being corrected on her posture. Gate on the sister looking twice.

13. GATE ON THE UNPAID BILL WHEN. Placing the paywall, normally between episodes 8 and 10 on a twelve-episode free block. DO. Sit the gate on an obligation that has been incurred and not yet collected. Three admissible shapes, and only three. The frozen decision: two things she wants or two she dreads, both named out loud, cut before she moves. The granted wish: she receives exactly what she has wanted since episode one, and the invoice has not arrived. The uncollected reveal: the largest fact in the free run lands and nothing has been done about it yet. One ban that all three share: never gate on a discharge. Not the explosion, not the rescue, not the answer. Required clause in all three cases: before the cut, someone names a specific coming event with a time and a place. The results at nine. Ring three at eight. The viewer must be able to picture the scene she is paying for. STOPS. If you gate on the explosion the tension is already spent and the first paid episode opens on aftermath. If you gate on unshaped dread the viewer cannot say what she would be watching next, and the gate reads as a stop sign instead of a door. BOUNDARY. What the granted wish obliges next is INVENTION MOVES (script) 2. This rule owns the placement. EXAMPLE. Before: free run ends on the warehouse fire, sirens, he runs in. After: free run ends with the owner handing her the deed to the diner, and her asking what time his wife gets in. Paid episode one opens on the wife's car in the lot at eight.

14. PAY THE BUYER FIRST WHEN. The first paid episode. DO. Rank every planned reveal by how much it changes the hero's standing. Put the largest in the final free episode, unresolved. Put the second largest in the first paid episode, and resolve it inside that episode. Never place two same-size reveals back to back anywhere in the run. STOPS. Either the free run peaks early and nobody pays, or the buyer pays and the first paid episode delivers less than the free one, which produces refunds and abandonment at the exact point you were paid. EXAMPLE (curling club). Before: ep 9 free, Dana learns the rink is being sold. Ep 10 paid, Dana calls a lawyer. After: ep 9 free, the signature on the sale is her dead husband's. Ep 10 paid, he walks into the club and the funeral was staged for the payout.

PART FOUR. THE OPPONENT.

15. GIVE THE OPPONENT THE RULEBOOK WHEN. Designing the antagonist for any series past twenty episodes. DO. Hand them authority over the terms rather than a capacity for violence. The trust, the visa sponsorship, the licensing board, the custody agreement, the family group chat. Pressure arrives as compliance, not as threat. STOPS. A villain who can be solved by one good punch ends the series around episode twelve and leaves the back half inventing henchmen. BOUNDARY. The one-time turn where an institution absolves the antagonist and hands them standing is INVENTION MOVES (script) 8. This is the standing build, not the turn. EXAMPLE (food truck). Before: the rival sends two men to smash the window. After: the rival sits on the licensing board and moves the vendor lottery to Tuesdays, when the truck is required by permit to be at the depot.

16. THREE PRESSURES, THREE DIRECTIONS WHEN. Cast planning, or any stretch where the same antagonist would otherwise appear in every episode. DO. Build three opponents who attack different parts of the same flaw. One holds money or status. One holds love or family loyalty. One holds the truth about the past. Each must be able to act alone inside a single ninety-second scene, and each must want something from the other two. STOPS. Otherwise one villain has to be present constantly, pacing dies the moment they are offscreen, and every episode replays the same attack at the same volume. BOUNDARY. Three or more pursuers sorted by incompatible reasons for wanting one target is INVENTION MOVES (script) 11. This sorts by which part of the flaw each one holds. Use both and you have your web. EXAMPLE (veterinary). Before: every episode, Ruth and the clinic owner fight again about the same surgery. After: ep 4 the owner freezes her licence, ep 5 her sister files for custody, ep 6 the kennel tech says he kept the anaesthesia log.

17. LET THE PLAN WORK FIRST WHEN. Any arc where the lead and her allies move against the opponent. DO. Run the plan to visible success, close enough to done that the viewer banks it as a win, then reveal the opponent permitted it. The room was emptied on purpose. The file was a copy. The ally reported in weeks ago. STOPS. A plan that fails on contact costs the viewer nothing to watch fail. The gap between a banked win and its cancellation is the material a cliffhanger is made of. EXAMPLE. Before: she reaches for the safe, guards take her arms, the opponent laughs. After: she opens the safe, photographs the deed, reaches the car, and her phone shows the photos already sent from her own account an hour ago.

PART FIVE. THE LONG RUN AND ITS OBJECTS.

18. STATE THE RULE, FLIP THE ROOM WEEKLY WHEN. Locking a lead for a run of sixty episodes or more, and building the grid. DO. Write her operating rule as one line of dialogue she can plausibly say, and have her say it inside the first two episodes. Then change who holds power in the room every single episode, and change what she believes about herself only at wide fixed intervals, roughly every tenth. Stage each of those as one scene where someone she cannot dismiss says the opposite to her face. She says the rule again the episode she breaks it. STOPS. With no stated rule, per-episode reversals have nothing to reverse against and every episode delivers the same volume of noise. Shifting the belief weekly makes her a weathervane and dissolves the long question. Never shifting it produces sixty variations of one scene. EXAMPLE. Before: she acts guarded around the new surgeon for twenty episodes. After: ep 2, "I fix what I can reach. Past that is not mine." Eps 11 to 20 she loses every room and holds the position. Ep 21, her opponent's mother says the thing her own mother never did. Ep 31, at the ambulance bay: "I fix what I can reach." She gets in.

19. PUT ONE INDIVISIBLE OBJECT ON THE TABLE WHEN. Any episode whose conflict is insult, contempt or humiliation. DO. Place one physical indivisible thing in the room that both parties need inside the next ninety seconds. The signed page, the last seat, the test result, the phone, the key. Whoever holds it holds the scene. STOPS. Without it the episode is cruelty with no clock. Nothing can be won or lost by the exit, so there is no reason to tap forward. EXAMPLE (baking). Before: her mother-in-law tells the guests the bread was store-bought. After: her mother-in-law holds the judges' entry form over the running tap. "Withdraw, or it goes in."

20. HANG THE BELIEF ON AN OBJECT WHEN. Her position has to survive a three-second recap. DO. Attach it to one thing you can frame at chest height in vertical: the ring she will not remove, the second phone, the unopened envelope. Movements of the object carry the interior beat and no dialogue explains it. STOPS. Without an object every recap re-explains in dialogue, spending the seconds that were supposed to buy the next hook. BOUNDARY. The recurring insert whose meaning is withheld until its last appearance is CRAFT RULES 17. This object's meaning is known from the start, which is the entire point: it is legible in one second. EXAMPLE. Before: voiceover reminds viewers she still has not forgiven her brother. After: the unopened envelope goes back in the glovebox at the end of every episode. In forty-four she opens it on camera and does not read it aloud.

PART SIX. THE FRAME.

21. ONE THING MOVES WHEN. Staging any frame that has to carry a plot beat in 9:16. DO. Let exactly one element change at the instant the beat lands. Hold everything else still, camera included. STOPS. Attention has a hard ceiling, and past it the extra elements are not blurred, they are deleted. Two simultaneous changes in a tall narrow frame means the audience misses the one you needed, and a beat nobody saw did not happen. EXAMPLE (large animal vet). Before: the vet argues with the owner, the herd mills behind them, a truck reverses through the gate, the son runs across frame with a bucket. After: the vet and the owner stand still. Nothing else moves. Behind them, one calf goes down.

22. PUT THE INTERRUPTER OFFSCREEN WHEN. A scene has to deliver information and two faces already fill the frame. DO. Add a third party who wants something incompatible and keep them out of frame. A car horn, a knock, a nurse paging the wrong name, a phone ringing with one name on it. The information gets delivered against the interruption, in pieces, three words per attempt. STOPS. A third body in 9:16 forces a wide shot and costs you both faces. With no interrupter the exposition plays clean, and clean exposition is where the swipe happens. BOUNDARY. Which beats may be interrupted, and the one-per-episode ration, is CRAFT RULES 3. This rule is about frame economy during exposition and does not spend that ration. EXAMPLE. Before: two people in an office. She explains her mother's diagnosis. He nods. After: she explains it while the intercom keeps paging her downstairs and he keeps hitting hold.
